## 1. Delivery platform restrictions: box/uniform ownership, third-party ads, need to partner with platforms/3PLs

### Takeaway
This is the most important finding. The exact model, LED "Smart Delivery Boxes" on food-delivery fleets, is already being run in the Gulf by Flyby (UAE/German) through **platform-level deals**: Deliveroo in Abu Dhabi (pilot), Noon in the UAE, Talabat in Dubai (June 2025) and, as of Nov 2026, Yango Ads. Ex-Talabat CEO Tomaso Rodriguez is Flyby's chairman. The precedent is therefore platform/fleet partnership, not paying individual riders. A rider-direct model in Kuwait would likely collide with platform branding control, 3PL contracts and an incumbent with Talabat ties.

### Inferences
- Platform-branded boxes (Talabat orange, Keeta yellow, etc.) are platform/3PL-supplied kit that is subject to "rider equipment compliance". A rider swapping or overlaying a box with a third-party screen would very likely breach kit standards in the 3PL contract and risk deactivation. A rider-direct model therefore puts the rider's livelihood at risk and creates tortious-interference exposure for the startup. (Inference; no Talabat rider terms text found.)
- Talabat now monetises ads at roughly 3.4% of GMV and has already chosen Flyby for Dubai. Talabat is likely to see a third-party screen on its riders as competing with, and free-riding on, its brand and fleet. Expect refusal or demands for exclusivity or revenue share. Commercially, the realistic routes are: (a) partner with platforms (possibly displacing or complementing Flyby in Kuwait/Egypt, where no Flyby deployment was found); (b) partner with 3PL fleet companies, which own vehicles and may want new revenue as Decision 109/2026's commission caps squeeze per-order rates; (c) target independent and in-house restaurant fleets, grocery/pharmacy/courier fleets, and multi-app freelancers, where boxes are unbranded or owned by the rider.
- Competitive implication: Flyby has a 1–2 year head start in the UAE, a patented box, platform relationships (Talabat, Deliveroo, Noon), a Talabat-connected chairman, a Series A in progress and a programmatic partner (Yango Ads). Kuwait is Talabat's home market and probably a natural Flyby expansion target.
- Multi-homing riders (Talabat plus Keeta plus Jahez) on shared boxes may face conflicting branding rules from each platform.

- Talabat Ads' off-app/DOOH inventory terms, and whether the Flyby deal is exclusive, are unknown.

## 2. Business model precedents and regulatory problems worldwide

### Takeaway
Rooftop car screens (Firefly, Vugo) and vinyl wraps (Wrapify, Carvertise) pay drivers roughly US$200–400 a month. They ran into taxi-regulator and city bans (NYC TLC vs Vugo; an LA proposal to ban digital rooftop ads). Delivery-box LED displays are a commodity hardware category from Chinese vendors, around US$1,500 per unit. In the UK, mobile digital ads are generally treated as vehicles, not planning-controlled signs, but must not dazzle or distract. London's fixed roadside digital guidance bans moving images.

### Cited Findings
- Rideshare advertising: drivers contract with ad companies that control the displays — [Wikipedia: Rideshare advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rideshare_advertising)
- Firefly raised a US$30M Series A for vehicle-mounted (rooftop) smart screens, primarily for Uber/Lyft drivers. It installs and maintains screens at no cost. Drivers earn "up to $300 per month". It reportedly requires 40+ hours a week over a 3-week evaluation — [PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/news/articles/firefly-closes-30m-series-a-seeks-to-offer-supplemental-ridesharing-income); [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/firefly-closes-30m-series-seeks-050000492.html); [The Rideshare Guy](https://therideshareguy.com/best-rideshare-advertising-companies/)
- The LA City Council considered banning digital rooftop advertising on taxi/rideshare vehicles. Outcome not confirmed — [The Rideshare Guy](https://therideshareguy.com/la-city-council-ban-digital-rooftop-advertising/)
- The NYC Taxi and Limousine Commission had barred drivers from using screens for advertising. A court ruled for Vugo that the TLC cannot ban the technology [snippet; secondary] — [Wikipedia: Vugo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vugo_(company))
- Uber/Lyft car ads coming to San Francisco (precedent of city-level debate) — [SF Chronicle](https://www.sfchronicle.com/business/article/Uber-Lyft-car-ads-headed-for-San-Francisco-12810742.php)
- Wrapify drivers earn roughly US$300–400 a month (per CEO). Digital displays earn about US$200–300 a month. Carvertise operates in 80+ metros (vendor blog) — [Triple Digital](https://tripledigital.com/get-paid-to-drive-the-ultimate-guide-to-car-wrap-advertising/); [cartopadvertisement](https://www.cartopadvertisement.com/blog/rideshare-advertising-uber-lyft)

### Inferences
- The Flyby model of still images, per-minute billing and platform deals looks designed to pre-empt the "moving images/distraction" objection seen in London guidance and US cases. A new entrant should adopt still-image-only, no-animation-while-moving and auto-dimming as baseline.
- Hardware is not a moat (commodity Chinese boxes). The defensible assets are platform/fleet access, regulatory approvals and measurement.

## 3. Product safety: power, lithium batteries, heat, certifications, IP rating, brightness

### Takeaway
GCC low-voltage electrical products fall under the GSO Low Voltage Technical Regulation (BD-142004-01, G-Mark), enforced in Kuwait by PAI through KUCAS. Lithium batteries fall under SASO's Electric Batteries TR, using IEC 62133-2 (adopted as GSO IEC 62133-2:2021). No public nit limit for mobile ad screens in the GCC was found. Gulf heat and battery safety are engineering risks that need a design response, not just compliance.

### Cited Findings
- Kuwait: PAI applies the G-Mark regime through KUCAS. Electrical standards are based on GSO standards and IEC standards with Kuwaiti deviations. For GSO "List 2" low-voltage products, a valid Gulf Type Examination Certificate from a GSO Notified Body is needed before a Technical Inspection Report (TIR) is issued. G-Mark for LV products has been enforced since 1 July 2016. Sources are dated, so confirm with PAI — [TÜV Rheinland KUCAS guide](https://www.tuv.com/content-media-files/kuwait/pdfs/1012-approvals-for-the-middle-east/tuv-rheinland-exporter-guideline-kucas.pdf); [Intertek – Gulf TR LV to Kuwait](https://www.intertek.com/government/export-import/regulatory-news-updates/news-kuwait/regulatory-updates-gulf-tr-electrical/); [Intertek – G-Mark update](https://www.intertek.com/government/export-import/regulatory-news-updates/news-kuwait/update-kuwait-gmark-toys-electricals/)
- GSO LV regulation: List 1 uses self-declaration with the Gulf Conformity Tracking Symbol (GCTS). List 2 requires Notified Body certification — [Cotecna GSO LV white paper](https://www.cotecna.com/media/f35lsvtd/gso-white-paper-062822.pdf); [SASO guidance on Gulf TR for LV](https://www.saso.gov.sa/ar/Laws-And-Regulations/Technical_regulations/Documents/mainDoc.pdf); [GSO customs guidance](https://www.gso.org.sa/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Guidance-for-customs-and-market-surveillance-inspectors-.pdf); [Spilma G-Mark guide](https://www.spilma.com/en/guides/gcc-kuwait-bahrain-qatar-oman-certification)
- Saudi: the SASO Technical Regulation for Electric Batteries (approved Sept 2018) lists SASO-IEC-62133-2 for secondary lithium cells/batteries — [SASO TR Electric Batteries PDF](https://saso.gov.sa/en/Laws-And-Regulations/Technical_regulations/Documents/TR-Electric-Batteries.pdf)
- GSO adopted IEC 62133-2 as GSO IEC 62133-2:2021 (approved 1 July 2021). It covers safety of portable sealed secondary lithium cells under intended use and foreseeable misuse — [GSO store](https://www.gso.org.sa/store/standards/GSO:789526/GSO%20IEC%2062133-2:2021?lang=en)
- Per a consultancy (verify via SABER): portable lithium batteries need SASO IEC 62133-2 under a mandatory CoC. Energy storage falls under SASO IEC 62619:2022. IECEE CB test reports can be reused — [GDES guide](https://en.gdestl.com/855.html)
- Lithium battery transport (relevant to importing boxes with built-in packs) follows the IATA Lithium Battery Guidance (01/01/2026 edition) — [IATA](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/05e6d8742b0047259bf3a700bc9d42b9/lithium-battery-guidance-document.pdf)
- Deliveroo's Abu Dhabi pilot used still images with road-safety creative guidelines. Flyby markets its box as "Road Safety Compliant" [snippet] — [Zawya](https://www.zawya.com/en/press-release/companies-news/deliveroo-transforms-bikes-into-digital-billboards-in-abu-dhabi-pilot-with-flyby-506339); [Flyby](https://flyby.global/product/)

### Inferences
- Likely compliance stack for a box screen: (1) the display/controller as AV/ICT equipment, under IEC 62368-1 as the usual safety standard; (2) the battery pack under IEC 62133-2 (cells and pack) plus UN 38.3 for transport; (3) EMC; (4) G-Mark/GCTS for GCC under the LV TR; (5) SABER CoC in KSA; (6) a KUCAS/PAI TIR in Kuwait; (7) in Egypt, GOEIC import registration and EOS standards. Items 1, 3 and 7 come from general background knowledge and were not verified in this session.
- If the screen draws on the motorcycle's 12V system rather than its own battery, it becomes a vehicle electrical modification. That has insurance and registration implications (see section 4). A separate battery adds thermal-runaway risk at 50°C ambient plus solar load on a box. Typical Li-ion charge limits are about 45°C, so thermal management, cell choice (e.g. LFP) and shading are design-critical. (Inference/background.)
- Shop-window screens are mains-powered fixed equipment, so the same LV G-Mark/KUCAS route applies. Glass-mounted LED film faces a heat-gain issue in sun-facing windows.

### Gaps
- No GCC, Egypt or Jordan nit or brightness limit for vehicle-mounted digital ads was found. No published spec for Flyby's brightness, IP rating or power source was found (product page not fetchable).
- Whether the GSO LV TR classifies LED advertising displays and battery packs as List 1 or List 2 is not confirmed.
- Egypt (EOS/GOEIC) and Jordan (JSMO) electrical/battery conformity routes were not researched in this session.
- No documented incidents of delivery-box battery fires in the Gulf were found.

## 4. Insurance: accident liability, product liability, motor insurance and vehicle modification

### Takeaway
Little region-specific evidence was found. Vendors themselves flag that LED boxes affect vehicle-modification disclosure, goods-in-transit, theft and public liability cover. Talabat has been upgrading rider insurance in the UAE, which suggests platform and 3PL insurance programmes would need to accommodate the device.

### Cited Findings
- LED delivery-box suppliers note the display "may affect vehicle modification disclosure, goods-in-transit cover, theft risk, public liability", especially where the vehicle is used for paid advertising as well as delivery — [Dynamo LED Displays](https://dynamo-led-displays.co.uk/delivery-box-outdoor-led-takeaway-box-display/)
- Talabat improved insurance coverage benefits for UAE delivery riders (Sept 2025). This indicates rider insurance runs through platform/3PL programmes [snippet] — [Gulfbase](https://gulfbase.com/news/talabat-improves-insurance-coverage-benefits-for-delivery-riders-in-uae/212018)

### Inferences
- Three layers of exposure: (a) motor third-party liability. An undisclosed modification or commercial-advertising use could let a motor insurer dispute cover. (b) Product liability for the screen/battery (fire, detachment, electric shock), which the startup should insure as the producer/importer. (c) Public/general liability for distraction claims, which are hard to prove but reputationally damaging. Platform/3PL partnership makes it possible to endorse fleet motor policies centrally. A rider-direct model leaves each rider's insurance uncertain.
- The startup should require partner indemnities in contracts, hold product liability plus public liability cover, and add rider personal accident top-ups.

### Gaps
- Kuwait/Egypt motor insurance policy wording on aftermarket electrical modifications and advertising use was not found.
- No case law found on liability for accidents attributed to vehicle-mounted digital ads in GCC/Egypt.

## 5. Audience measurement and privacy (GPS, cameras, Wi-Fi/Bluetooth sniffing)

### Takeaway
GPS-based exposure modelling is the established approach (Flyby uses location-triggered ads with per-minute billing). Cameras or MAC-address sniffing would trigger personal-data rules. Egypt's PDPL (Law 151/2020) became enforceable through its 2025 Executive Regulations, with a licensing regime and a 1 Nov 2026 compliance deadline. Kuwait's CITRA regulation is narrower (sources disagree on scope), but rider GPS tracking still raises employment and contractual consent issues.

### Cited Findings
- Flyby boxes are 4G/5G connected, remotely updated and show geo-targeted, location-based ads. Noon's advertisers are billed by minutes displayed [snippet] — [Zawya (Talabat–Flyby)](https://www.zawya.com/en/press-release/companies-news/talabat-partners-with-flyby-to-introduce-smart-delivery-boxes-in-dubai-yaxrd856); [Menabytes](https://www.menabytes.com/noon-digital-ads-fleet/)
- Egypt PDPL (Law 151/2020) is GDPR-modelled and applies to processing of Egyptian residents' data regardless of the controller's location. Its Executive Regulations (Ministerial Decree 816 of 2025, issued Nov 2025) introduce licensing, with a compliance deadline of 1 Nov 2026 [secondary] — [Securiti](https://securiti.ai/privacy-laws/); [Kooch](https://kooch.co/en/post/middle-east-data-protection-laws-2026)
- Kuwait: CITRA Data Privacy Protection Regulation (Resolution 26/2024, Feb 2024). Sources conflict on scope. Some say it covers all CITRA-licensed service providers that process personal data. Chambers 2026 says it applies only to CITRA-licensed telecom/internet providers. Breach-notice deadlines are cited as 24h or 72h. There is no general GDPR-style private-sector statute — [Al Tamimi](https://www.tamimi.com/news/kuwait-data-privacy-protection-regulations/); [Chambers 2026 Kuwait](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/data-protection-privacy-2026/kuwait); [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiperdataprotection.com/?t=law&c=KW); [ConsentStack](https://www.consentstack.io/regulations/kw-dppr)
- Kuwait Decision 109/2026 requires delivery platforms to protect consumer data and keep records. This is relevant if measurement relies on platform order data — [ARY News](https://arynews.tv/kuwait-issues-new-rules-for-e-delivery-platforms)

### Inferences
- The lowest-risk measurement design: GPS trace of the box (disclosed to and consented by the rider/3PL), joined with third-party footfall/traffic density models. Avoid cameras and device sniffing in Egypt, KSA (PDPL) and the UAE (PDPL) unless anonymised on-device. Shop-window screens with cameras face the same issue.
- Rider location data is personal data of the rider. In Egypt it would need lawful-basis, licensing and cross-border transfer analysis under PDPL.

### Gaps
- Saudi PDPL, UAE PDPL, Bahrain, Oman, Qatar and Jordan (Law 24/2023) were not separately verified in this session.
- No GCC regulator guidance on Wi-Fi/MAC sniffing for advertising was found.

## 6. Advertising content standards across the region

### Takeaway
Only UAE sources were verified in this session. The UAE's 2025 Media Law regime imposes 20 content standards with fines up to AED 1M, an advertiser-permit requirement (Decree-Law 42/2025) and a ban on alcohol including surrogate ads. Comparable cultural/religious content rules, Arabic-language requirements and prohibited categories (alcohol, pork, gambling, tobacco/vape, dating, some medical/financial claims) should be assumed for Kuwait, KSA (GCAM) and Egypt but were not verified here.

### Cited Findings
- UAE Media Law effective 29 May 2025. The Media Council set 20 mandatory content standards across media including digital. Maximum fine for a content-standard breach is AED 1 million. Sources conflict on the legal basis (Federal Decree-Law 55/2023 cited) and on regulator naming — [Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/uae/uae-media-law-media-must-follow-20-key-standards-to-avoid-fines-of-up-to-dh1m-1.500157204); [Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=6ce714d9-e847-460c-87bb-3426a7144dd6)
- Ads must be factual, not misleading and clearly identified as advertising — [Blackstone Services](https://www.blackstoneservices.ae/blog/uae-advertising-content-standards-guide); [inBeat](https://inbeat.agency/blog/dubai-legal-advertising-guidelines)
- Under Decree-Law 42/2025, advertisers (individuals, influencers, businesses) need a UAE Media Council advertiser permit before publishing ads on digital platforms. One source reports it is free until 2028 with fines up to AED 1M — [Al Tamimi](https://www.tamimi.com/law-update/technology-edition/articles/uae-introduces-mandatory-advertiser-permit-for-content-creators/); [QAS Pro](https://qasproglobal.com/uaes-new-advertiser-permit-rule-what-it-means-for-brands-influencers-and-businesses/); [Encore MENA](https://encore-mena.com/insights/uae-advertising-influencer-regulations/)
- UAE alcohol advertising is prohibited, including surrogate/"alibi" ads — [UpScale Digital](https://upscale-digital.com/blog/advertising-rules-in-the-uae/)

### Inferences
- The operator should run a pre-clearance content policy aligned to the strictest market (KSA/Kuwait). It should include an Arabic-first or bilingual requirement, no alcohol, pork, gambling or tobacco/vape, modesty standards for imagery, no political/religious/sectarian content, regulated-sector claims (health, finance, real estate) checked against sector licences, and Ramadan sensitivity. It should also include road-safety creative rules (still images, large type, no QR codes requiring rider/driver interaction, no imitation of traffic signs).
- Advertisers whose ads run on the screens may need their own permits (e.g. the UAE advertiser permit, if screens are treated as "digital platforms"). This is uncertain.

### Gaps
- Kuwait Ministry of Information / municipality ad-content rules, Saudi GCAM advertising licensing and content code, Egypt Supreme Council for Media Regulation and Jordan rules were not retrieved in this session. Other researchers or follow-up are needed.
